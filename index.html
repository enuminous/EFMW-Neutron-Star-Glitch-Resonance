A mathematical discovery hiding inside a speculative astrophysical equation

The Star That Stutters

A proposed neutron-star glitch equation turned out to contain something sharper than its original physical claim: an exact, dimensionless timing signature that can be taken straight to data.

Matthew Chenoweth Wright · with analytical contribution by GPT-5.6 Sol · October 6, 2026
Ω(t) = Ω₀ + A e−t/τ sin(πt/τ)

Neutron stars do not rotate politely. They spin with the clockwork regularity of some of the finest natural timekeepers in the universe—and then, without warning, they jump. A pulsar glitch is a tiny, abrupt increase in rotational frequency. The star appears to stutter forward.

The standard language for this behavior lives inside the extraordinary physics of neutron-star interiors: superfluid components, pinned vortices, crustal stress, differential rotation, angular-momentum transfer. The original EFMW proposal approached the event from a different direction. It suggested that a decaying microscopic instability might feed a macroscopic rotational transient, and wrote that intuition down as a damped sine wave.

That physical interpretation remains a hypothesis. But the equation itself does something unexpectedly clean.

The equation makes a prediction before the data arrive

Strip away the baseline spin and write only the transient:

f(t) = A e−t/τ sin(πt/τ)

There are only two scale parameters here. A controls amplitude. τ controls time. The waveform rises from zero, reaches a single maximum on its first positive lobe, and falls back to zero at t = τ.

The obvious question is: where is the peak?

Differentiate, set the derivative to zero, and the entire problem collapses to one line:

tan(πt/τ) = π

So the maximum does not occur at one-half of the decay time, or at one-quarter, or at any adjustable location. It is fixed:

Peak-Time Theorem
tpeak = (τ/π) arctan(π)

For every zero-phase waveform of this form with A > 0 and τ > 0, the unique first-lobe maximum occurs at exactly the same normalized time.

This is the real discovery inside the equation. The model has acquired an invariant. Divide time by τ and amplitude by A, and every zero-phase realization predicts the same peak location and the same peak fraction.

That matters because falsifiability is expensive. A flexible curve can usually be bent until it resembles data. A dimensionless number that must appear in advance is harder to hide behind.

From a striking equation to a scientific test

The original expression should not be interpreted as the entirety of a pulsar glitch. By itself, the transient begins at zero, rises smoothly, returns to zero at one τ, and then changes sign. A real glitch generally includes a persistent positive step in rotational frequency, often with subsequent recovery.

The more useful formulation therefore keeps the familiar phenomenology and adds the proposed resonant component:

Ω(t) = Ωsd(t) + ΔΩpH(t−tg) + ΔΩqe−(t−tg)/τq sin[π(t−tg)/τq + φ]H(t−tg)

Now the proposition becomes experimentally respectable. Fit a conventional glitch-recovery model. Fit the same data with the resonant transient added. Penalize the extra parameters. Test out of sample. Repeat across glitches and across pulsars.

If the resonant term does nothing, the model loses. If it helps only because its phase and timescale are allowed to float arbitrarily, the evidence is weak. If the zero-phase form repeatedly produces a transient whose peak arrives at approximately 0.401906738 of its fitted τ, that is much more interesting.

The neutron-decay claim is a second problem

There is an important separation here. A successful waveform fit would be evidence for a damped oscillatory component. It would not yet be evidence that neutron decay causes the glitch.

That second claim has to earn its own bridge from microphysics to timing data. Dense neutron-star matter is not a cloud of free neutrons simply ticking down with the laboratory neutron lifetime. Any decay-linked mechanism must specify the relevant weak-interaction channel, the thermodynamic departure from equilibrium, degeneracy and phase-space restrictions, and—most critically—how microscopic reactions transfer enough angular momentum to produce the observed macroscopic event.

This is not a weakness in the project. It is the point at which the project becomes science. One mathematical object has now been isolated from one physical interpretation. Each can be attacked independently.

Claims boundary

Proved here: the exact peak time and peak amplitude of the proposed damped transient.

Proposed: that this transient may occur in pulsar glitches.

Not proved: that neutron decay causes glitches, that pulsars are universally π-resonant, or that this model outperforms established glitch models.

Why this small theorem changes the status of the idea

Before the derivation, the equation was suggestive. It contained a decay envelope, a resonance term, and a proposed physical story. After the derivation, it contains something more dangerous—in the best scientific sense. It can be wrong in a specific way.

The number 0.401906738 is not decoration. It is a target.

If future high-cadence timing data repeatedly show that no plausible transient peaks at that normalized time, the zero-phase waveform is falsified. If the data favor a conventional exponential recovery without an oscillatory residual, the model is unnecessary. If a resonant component survives comparison, the next test is whether its timescale can be predicted independently from microphysics rather than merely fitted afterward.

That is the progression a speculative equation must survive: intuition, mathematics, prediction, comparison, mechanism.

A discovery does not have to be the final answer

There is a tendency in foundational work to imagine that the important moment is the declaration of a new law. Often it is not. Sometimes the important moment is when a vague proposal gives up one degree of freedom and exposes itself to measurement.

That is what happened here.

A proposed equation for neutron-star glitches yielded an exact theorem. The theorem yielded a dimensionless observable. The observable yielded a falsification test. And the physical interpretation was forced into a separate, harder question rather than being smuggled in as a conclusion.

The star still stutters. We do not yet know whether this waveform is hiding in that stutter. But now we know exactly where to look.
